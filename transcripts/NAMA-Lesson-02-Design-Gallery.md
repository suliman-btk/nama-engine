# NAMA — Lesson 2: Designing a Clear Lesson

**Audience:** Teachers
**Lesson outcome:** Plan a short lesson with a clear goal, small steps, a quick check and a strong close.

## Pure narration

Welcome

In this lesson we look at how to design a clear lesson. We start with goals, then we plan the steps, then we check understanding, and finally we close the lesson well.

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to write a clear learning goal, plan a lesson in small steps, and check what your learners understand.

As Antoine de Saint-Exupéry is often quoted: a goal without a plan is just a wish. A clear goal tells you where the lesson is going.

Remember this: a good goal is specific, observable, and achievable.

A strong lesson rests on three pillars: clear goals, active practice, and honest feedback.

Planning a lesson has two parts. The first part is content, which covers the key ideas and the examples. The second part is activities, which covers practice and discussion.

Here is a case. A teacher had a mixed class of beginners and experts. The challenge was that half the class felt bored. The teacher solved it by giving extension tasks to the experts.

Let's work through an example. A lesson lasts sixty minutes. Ten minutes go to the warm up. Thirty minutes go to practice. Ten minutes go to feedback. That leaves ten minutes for the summary.

A lesson plan is like a map. The learning goal is the destination, and each activity is a road that leads there.

Here is a tip. Ask one question at a time, and wait at least three seconds for an answer.

Many people believe that longer lessons teach more. In fact, short and focused lessons keep attention. Another myth is that learners remember what they hear. In fact, they remember what they practise.

Group work has clear benefits. It builds teamwork, and it lets learners teach each other. But it also has costs. It takes more time, and some learners may stay quiet.

Let's compare a lecture, a discussion, and a project. A lecture is fast, a discussion is slower, and a project is the slowest. A lecture needs little preparation, a discussion needs some, and a project needs the most. A lecture is not very active, a discussion is partly active, and a project is fully active.

Keep your videos short. One large study of online courses found that learners stayed engaged with videos up to six minutes long.

Imagine a school survey where 80 percent of teachers plan their goals first, and 45 percent check understanding in every lesson.

Now imagine a class that practises every week. In week one, the average score is 50. In week two, it is 58. In week three, it is 67. And in week four, it reaches 75. Practice adds up.

Quick check. Which goal is easiest to observe? Is it A, understand fractions? B, add two fractions with the same denominator? Or C, appreciate maths? The answer is B, because you can see learners do it.

True or false? Feedback works best when it comes weeks later. That is false: feedback works best when it is quick.

Let's sum up. Start with a clear goal. Teach in small steps. Check understanding often. And give quick feedback.

Your next step: choose one lesson you teach this week, rewrite its goal, and add one quick check.
